
## Ten programs instead of four

Tier 1 of the program catalog is the ten every contractor is asked for
regardless of trade. Four existed. The other six now do: Job Safety Analysis,
Safety Training & Orientation, Disciplinary & Enforcement, Safety Policy,
Injury & Illness Recordkeeping, and Drug & Alcohol.

### A shared spine, and why the original four do not use it

Open any real safety program and the first two sections and the last three are
close to interchangeable — purpose, scope, who looks after it, what employees
are told, where it is kept, when it is reviewed. Writing fifty-six variants of
"Availability of This Program" adds nothing and guarantees they drift. So
`spine.ts` owns the frame and each program owns its subject.

The original four are deliberately not retrofitted. They are released,
customers hold versions of them, and re-assembling released prose to share a
helper would change the bytes of documents somebody has already submitted to a
hiring client, for no benefit to them.

### The two written most carefully

**Drug & Alcohol** is an employment policy, not a safety procedure, and that
is where a generated document can do real legal harm: testing law varies by
state, several states restrict action over lawful off-duty conduct, and
commercial drivers sit under a separate federal regime. So it states the
commitment — which is genuinely the company's to make and the part a hiring
client is asking about — and refers the mechanics of testing to counsel, the
client's contract, and the law where the work is done. It names no substance,
no cut-off, no panel, and no consequence expressed as a legal entitlement.

**Recordkeeping** sits next to rules about which injuries go on a log and
which events are reportable on a clock. It restates none of them. It is a
procedure — who decides, by when, against what — and it says in the document
that it is not a substitute for the current rules. A contractor whose program
instead contains a summary of the rules is carrying a summary that will go
stale without telling them.

**Disciplinary** is the one most often written badly. Platforms ask for it and
most contractors supply a ladder of punishments, which is worse than nothing:
a company that reliably punishes people for what goes wrong stops hearing
about what goes wrong, and every other program depends on people reporting
things. It draws the distinction that matters — honest mistake, judgment call,
deliberate choice to skip a control — and only the last is a discipline matter.

### One test file instead of ten

`every-program.test.mts` runs the shared invariants against the registry,
assembling each program twice with its options on and off. Adding a program
puts it under all of them automatically, rather than being as safe as whoever
remembered to copy the checks across.

It immediately found three defects in **already-released** programs:

- HazCom's `matchesLabel` no longer recognized its own configured title, so a
  gap report naming "Hazard Communication" would never have offered to prepare
  it. Invisible from outside — the customer just never sees the offer.
- HazCom said the responsible role "records who has been trained", which reads
  as a claim about people rather than about the record.
- British spellings in documents going to US contractors: "labelled" through
  HazCom, "programme" in PPE and Incident Reporting, "organise" in the EAP.

Both matchers were widened to accept every title the requirement has carried,
and HazCom and PPE went to 1.1.0.

Two pieces of copy went stale the moment the fifth program landed — the
checkout said "all four written programs" and the documents page said only four
had generators. Both now read from the registry.

**315 + 12 tests pass, lint and build clean.**
