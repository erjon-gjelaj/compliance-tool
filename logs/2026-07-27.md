# 2026-07-27

## Task 032: OSHA citations, retrieved rather than recalled

The emails hedge every line with "our understanding, not law", and rightly
so — nothing in lib/requirements had a source anyone could check. This adds
one, without asserting anything from memory.

**How it works.** Candidates live in lib/requirements/citations.ts as
proposals, not claims. `npm run verify:citations` fetches each from the eCFR
API, takes the official section heading and the subpart description from the
ancestry endpoint, and keeps it only if the words in `expect` actually turn
up in what came back. What survives is written to verified-citations.json,
which is committed and is the only thing the application reads — the request
path never touches the network, so a review is deterministic and an eCFR
outage cannot reach a contractor.

The subpart matters more than expected. Two sections are published as plain
"General requirements" — 1910.132 and 1926.1203 — and only the subpart says
one is about protective equipment and the other about confined spaces in
construction. Checking the heading alone would have accepted them without
ever confirming the subject, and displaying it alone would have told the
reader nothing.

**Both failure modes were tested, not assumed.** A nonexistent section
(1910.9999) is refused as not found. A real section with the wrong
expectation (1910.147 against "asbestos") is refused with the retrieved
heading quoted back in the reason. The script exits non-zero on any drop,
because a citation list that quietly shrinks is exactly the sort of thing
that goes unnoticed for months.

Twelve citations verified across eight of the twelve requirements, against
Title 29 as current to 2026-07-16.

**What this deliberately does NOT do**, and it is the opposite of what I
expected going in. It does not move anything off `verified: false`. That flag
means a person has checked ISNetworld's or Avetta's own published
requirements — a completely different claim from "an OSHA standard exists on
this subject". lib/requirements warns that citing a regulation for a
contractual requirement is the most damaging mistake that file can make, and
flipping `source` to "osha" because a standard exists would have been exactly
that mistake. So source stays "platform", verified stays false, and the
citation sits alongside under "OSHA standards on this subject" — a reference
to take to a safety adviser, not a statement that they must hold a document.
Whether 1910 or 1926 applies to a given contractor turns on their work and
their site, and this software cannot determine that.

**One validator rule was removed on purpose.** Citations were restricted to
items with source "osha". That conflated two things: who is asking for a
document, and which standard covers the subject. Hazard communication is
both — ISN asks for the programme and 1910.1200 exists — and the rule forced
a choice between dropping a real reference and restating a contractual ask as
law. `supportsClaim` is still required. The reasoning is recorded inline so
nobody restores it as a tidy-up.

**Four requirements carry no citation, and that is the deliverable rather
than a gap:** written safety programme, drug and alcohol, training records,
insurance certificate. No single OSHA standard requires the document a
prequalification platform is asking for in those cases. They stay contractual
and the email keeps saying so.

Build, lint and typecheck clean; schema validation passes on a generated
review. scripts/ is excluded from the app typecheck since it runs through
tsx under different rules.

**Left for human review:** the candidate mappings themselves. Retrieval
proves a section exists and mentions the subject. It cannot prove the mapping
is the right one to put in front of a contractor — that judgement is still
a person's.

## Acting on the citation review

A review came back on the candidate mappings. All twelve section numbers were
confirmed correct. Three things changed as a result, and one point was
checked rather than taken on trust.

**The scope note was the substantive one, and it was verified before acting
on it.** The claim was that 1910.147 excludes construction employment.
Fetching the section text confirms it: "This standard does not cover the
following: (A) Construction and agriculture employment". 1910.146 says the
same. That matters here more than it might elsewhere — a scaffolding or
mechanical subcontractor on a plant turnaround may well be doing construction
work, and showing them a general industry standard with no caveat is close to
misleading.

Rather than hand-write the caveat, the verifier now detects it. It looks for
exclusion language that specifically names construction, which is narrower
than it sounds and deliberately so: 1910.1200 also contains "does not apply
to", but about hazardous waste, and a check for exclusion language alone
would have flagged it wrongly. Exactly the two sections that exclude
construction are flagged; 1910.1200 is not.

**Tightened every expectation** to the reviewer's suggestions - "control of
hazardous energy" plus "lockout/tagout" rather than the looser single term,
"duty to have fall protection", "criteria for personal protective equipment",
and so on. All twelve still verify, which is the useful part: a stricter test
that still passes is worth more than a loose one that always did.

**Documented the matching semantics**, which the review rightly flagged as
unclear. `expect` is a conjunction: every term must appear, matched against
the subpart description and section heading together. Body text is
deliberately not searched - a phrase can turn up anywhere in a long
regulation for reasons unrelated to its subject, and matching on that would
accept almost anything.

**One gap left open on purpose.** 1910.147 excludes construction, and this
file has no construction counterpart for lockout/tagout. Which 1926 standard
fills that role is a domain judgement, not a retrieval - the review itself
said it "may need additional construction-specific electrical, equipment, or
process standards", and guessing at one would be precisely the mistake this
pipeline exists to prevent. It stays empty until a person decides.

Also added tsx as a dev dependency so `npm run verify:citations` resolves.

## Making the refusal machine-verifiable

Second round of review, and the central point was right: an empty citation
array cannot distinguish "not researched" from "researched and deliberately
not mapped", and those need opposite responses from whoever reads it next.

`CITATION_GAPS` now declares the refusal as data — requirement, work context,
status `context-dependent`, stable code `CONTEXT_DEPENDENT_CITATION`, and a
reason. `resolveCitations()` fails closed on it: a declared gap returns no
citation at all rather than the nearest available one, and a request for
construction filters out any standard whose own text excludes construction.
Both matter because the tempting refactor is "we have exactly one
lockout/tagout citation, use it" — and that citation says in its own scope
clause that it does not cover construction.

`CONTEXTUAL_CITATION_CANDIDATES` is typed and empty on purpose. 1926.417 is a
real construction lockout provision, but it is about electrical circuits;
offering it as *the* construction answer would be the over-mapping the gap
exists to prevent. The shape lets a narrower verified mapping be added later
without anyone having to declare a universal counterpart to do it.

The gap is now visible in three places rather than one: the verifier,
tasks.json, and the review email under "Where we stop short".

**The project has tests for the first time.** node:test through tsx, no
framework, eight cases and mostly negative — because the failure worth
catching is not "the right citation is missing", it is "a real, relevant,
plausible section reaches a contractor it does not cover", which looks
correct in every review.

Two of them earn their place immediately. One asserts confined-space DOES
still resolve for construction, guarding against the refusal being
over-broad — 1910.146 also excludes construction, but 1926.1203 was verified,
so there is no gap to declare there. The other asserts 1926.417 does not
satisfy generic construction lockout/tagout.

Mutation-checked rather than assumed: removing the gap declaration fails the
test. Worth noting what did NOT fail — the two tests about 1910.147 still
passed, because the retrieved `excludesConstruction` flag blocks it
independently. Two mechanisms guard the same hole, and removing either alone
does not reopen it.

## Docs brought up to date

README described Scope A and a project with no tests. It now says where
things actually are, leads with the diff-not-judgement idea, and documents
`npm test` and `npm run verify:citations`.

CLAUDE.md said the eCFR check "runs on every submission, automatically". It
does not, deliberately, and that is now recorded as a decision rather than
left looking like a bug: fetching per submission would make identical input
produce different output depending on eCFR's uptime, put a third-party outage
between a contractor and their email, and add seconds to a function that has
already died twice on its wall clock. Also added the rules about declaring
refused mappings and keeping retrieved facts separate from mapping decisions,
and put `npm test` in the definition of done.
