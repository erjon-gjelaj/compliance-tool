## Directory badge (task 062)

Added the SaaS Browser listing badge to the footer, in the bottom row opposite
the copyright line. That row was already a single line of small print, so the
badge sits where attribution-shaped things belong rather than competing with
the nav or the ISN/Avetta disclaimer.

The artwork is served from `/public` rather than the issuer's CDN. It is a

update the artwork, ours does not follow.

## The money path (task 063)

Quoting, accepting, declining and being paid are now **events**, not columns.
The obvious implementation — a `quoted_amount` column and an `accepted`
boolean — would have reintroduced exactly the bug task 055 was written to
remove. A quote is not a property of a request; it is something that happened
to it, on a date, by somebody, and often more than once. A revised price is a
second quote, not an edit of the first, and editing in place would lose the
change that a customer is most likely to query.

So migration 0010 adds two amount columns and four kinds, with a database
constraint that an amount may exist **only** on a quote — otherwise a reply
could carry a number that no screen reads and no state reacts to, sitting in
the table looking authoritative.

An operator can now send a price, record a payment, and set a plan. That last
one is the write 0007 designed for and nothing ever performed: granting
somebody the thing they just paid for was a SQL-editor task until today. It
reports when no company profile matched rather than silently changing nothing,
because "I told them they were upgraded and they weren't" is the failure that
costs a customer.

The customer sees the price in their thread and accepts or declines in one
click. Acceptance is re-derived from the log on the server, so a price that
has been withdrawn, revised, or already answered in another tab cannot be
accepted from a stale page — the one mistake here that costs real money to
unpick.

Eleven new tests, all negative. The two worth naming: **acceptance is not
payment**, and **the agreed price is the one that was accepted, not a later
revision**. Both describe screens that would look completely reasonable and be
wrong about money.

## Program 2: Personal Protective Equipment (task 059)

Built on the pattern task 058 established. Seven questions, one conditional;
thirteen sections at base, fifteen at full branching. Renders to Word and PDF
from the same section tree.

The spine is the **written hazard assessment**, not an equipment list. Our own
reference data says of this requirement: *"Check it includes the written hazard
assessment, not just a kit list."* That is the difference between a PPE program
that is accepted and one that comes back — a list describes what a company
owns, an assessment describes how it decided, and the second is the part a
reviewer cannot infer. So four of the sections are about assessing rather than
about equipment, and the questions ask who walks the job and where the written
assessment lives.

It deliberately does **not** ask for a list of the equipment held. Same trap as
the chemical inventory in HazCom: a list typed in one afternoon is wrong by the
next job, and a program naming equipment the company no longer issues is worse
than one describing how equipment gets selected.

**Respiratory protection is excluded in words**, in the Scope section, rather
than by omission. Silence in a safety program is read as coverage, and a
respirator program needs fit testing and medical evaluation that none of these
questions ask about. Saying so is safer than saying nothing.

Nothing in it asserts that training has happened or that any particular
equipment is owned. Both are facts about the world on a given day that a form
cannot establish, so every training sentence is a commitment about what happens
before an employee is assigned work.

Nineteen tests, all negative. The ones worth naming:

- No section tells the reader what the law obliges them to do — the whole basis
  on which this file is allowed to exist.
- `sourceRef` never reaches the page.
- Site rules are described as a **floor rather than a ceiling**. The inversion
  is the dangerous one: a program promising to follow whatever the host asks
  would commit the company to less than its own assessment found.
- Who pays is what they told us. Claiming the company pays for everything when
  it does not is a false statement in a document they sign.

Verified by rendering a real document rather than trusting the section tree: a
six-page PDF and a Word file, both read back. That review found a British
"defence" sitting in prose written for US contractors — fixed, and now guarded
by a test covering nine British spellings.

**The release flag is `internal_testing` on purpose.** `docs/release-one.md`
says the flag is lifted per programme, by a person, once the prose has been
reviewed by somebody qualified. That review has not happened, and I am not the
person to do it. Changing one line to `customer_available` is the whole of
shipping it — and a test fails when it changes, so the lift has to be
deliberate rather than accidental.

One side effect worth noting: a generated document already in the database
carried `program_id = "personal_protective_equipment"` with no template behind
it. It now resolves to a real title instead of falling back to the humanised id.

## Program 3: Emergency Action Plan (task 060)

Seven questions, one conditional; thirteen sections at base, fifteen at full
branching.

The reference data says of this requirement: *"Site-specific detail is usually
what gets it sent back."* That is the whole problem with an emergency plan
written for a subcontractor. Most of their work happens on somebody else's
site, where the host's alarm, exits and muster point govern — so a plan
describing "our evacuation route" is describing a building the crew will not
be standing in.

A generic plan cannot be made site-specific by writing more prose. So the
document ends with a **Site Emergency Record**: a table with a row per site,
shipped deliberately blank, completed at orientation before work starts. The
plan says in as many words that a blank row is not an oversight — the company
does not know the arrangements of a site it has not been given, and this
document does not pretend otherwise. That blank table is the most useful thing
in it.

Scope states plainly that host arrangements govern and this plan does not
replace them; what it adds is how those arrangements get found out, how the
crew is told, and how the company accounts for its own people once outside.

### A renderer bug this found

`render-pdf` drew **no rules for tables at all**. It only advanced the cursor
between rows, which is invisible but harmless for a table whose cells carry
text — the words imply the grid. It falls apart for a table meant to be
written on: the blank rows were whitespace, and the form had no lines to fill
in. Word was unaffected, since `docx` draws its own borders.

No template had ever used a table before, so this was the first time the code
path ran. Now ruled under the header and under each row, wrap-aware so a
two-line heading does not cross its own rule. Verified by decompressing the
PDF content streams and counting the stroke operations: five rules, at the
right coordinates, spanning the full content width.

### The tests

Nineteen, all negative. The ones worth naming:

- It never claims a particular site's arrangements are known. Naming a muster
  point for a refinery nobody has visited is the expensive lie here.
- The site's own alarm takes precedence over any horn the company carries.
  The inversion — a crew listening for their own horn on a site with a real
  alarm — is the one that gets somebody hurt.
- Nobody is sent back in to look for a missing person.
- Accounting is **by name**, not by headcount, across all three methods. A
  headcount that happens to match is the classic way a missing person is
  missed. One of the three wordings failed this on first run and was rewritten.
- A contractor with neither premises nor client sites still gets a coherent
  plan. Both conditional sections drop out in that combination, and the guard
  is against an emergency plan containing nothing about evacuating.

Release is `internal_testing`. The prose has not been reviewed by anyone
qualified; one line ships it, and a test fails when that line changes.

## The trade axis, finally doing something (task 062-data)

I was wrong about one thing earlier and it is worth correcting: I said the
trade x platform promise needed structural work. It did not. `requirementsFor`
already filtered correctly — it handled case, and it deliberately refused to
narrow on "Both" or "Not sure" because neither is a platform name. The
machinery had been written long ago and had simply never had anything to do,
because all twelve entries said `trades: "all"`.

So this was purely a data job.

### Research

Public sources only: consultant RAVS checklists and the text of the OSHA
standards. The authoritative lists live behind an ISNetworld or Avetta login
and stay a human job — those are the ones that turn a candidate into a
verified entry.

The picture was consistent across sources: a core of roughly nine programs
that nearly every operator asks for, then three to eight more depending on
scope of work.

### What was added

Twelve entries became twenty-six.

Three core ones the research surfaced that we were missing entirely: fire
prevention, incident reporting and investigation, and a written safety
training program — which is a separate item from the training records we
already listed.

Eleven trade-specific ones, the first entries in the file that are not
`trades: "all"`:

| Trade | Now also gets |
| --- | --- |
| Electrical | electrical safety, arc flash, aerial lifts |
| Scaffolding | scaffolding safety, silica, hearing conservation |
| Welding / fabrication | hot work, welding & cutting, aerial lifts, hearing conservation |
| Insulation | asbestos, silica, lead, aerial lifts |
| Industrial cleaning | hydroblasting, silica, lead, hearing conservation |
| Other | core only |

Every one is still `verified: false`, so the output renders them as commonly
asked for rather than as something the contractor must hold. A new `sources`
field records where each candidate came from, with the note that a
consultant's blog is a lead and not evidence — it exists so verifying an entry
means opening the thing it was drawn from, and so an entry nobody can trace
can be deleted rather than inherited.

"Other" matches none of the trade-specific entries and receives the core set.
That is deliberate: handing somebody a scaffolding requirement because their
free-text trade contained a word we liked is exactly the over-matching this
file exists to prevent.

### Tests

Sixteen, and they are the first tests this data has ever had. The two that
matter most both guard silent failures:

- **Every trade named in the data is a real trade from the intake.**
  "Welding/fabrication" instead of "Welding / fabrication" matches nobody,
  forever, and nothing anywhere reports an error. The requirement simply
  never appears.
- **A substring is not a match.** "Scaffolding inspection consultancy" must
  not pick up the scaffolding requirement, because that is a trade nobody
  assessed.

Plus: no entry may claim OSHA as its source, nothing is verified until a
person says so, two different trades must not produce identical lists, and
"Both"/"Not sure" must never return fewer items than a named platform.

`REQUIREMENTS_VERSION` moved to `2026-09-07.1`, so every review produced from
here on records which edition of the reference data it was built against.

## Testing the matcher, and an operations page

### The matcher finally has tests

The code that decides what a contractor is told is missing had none, which
mattered more the moment the reference data went from twelve entries to
twenty-six and trade filtering went live — nothing was checking that the right
list reached the right person.

Eighteen tests, all negative. A crash in this file is harmless: the review
falls back to the generic explainer. A confident wrong answer is not, and it
looks exactly like a working review.

The ones worth reading:

- **Nothing readable means unknown, never `likely_missing`.** A list of things
  somebody is told they lack, derived from no document at all, is the worst
  output this product can produce.
- **OCR can show a subject is covered but never that one is absent**, and one
  unreadable file poisons the missing verdict for the whole review — the
  phrase could be in the part we could not read.
- A scaffolder's review contains no hot work item, and `"Other — scaffolding
  inspection consultancy"` is not guessed into the scaffolding requirement.
- The same submission produces the same review twice, which is the promise the
  whole no-LLM rule exists to keep.

All eighteen passed first run. Nothing was wrong with the matcher; it simply
had nothing guarding it.

### /internal now exists, and does two jobs

It used to 404 — there were pages beneath it and no index.

**Reviews that did not go out.** The analysis runs inside `after()`, so a
failure happens once the response has already gone: the contractor gets the
generic explainer, nothing raises an alarm, and the first anybody hears is
when they ask. Every run has been logged to the `analyses` table with its
error since the pipeline was built, and nothing had ever read those rows.

Deliberately not a third-party monitoring service. The data is already in the
database; one query beats an account, a DSN and a monthly bill at this volume.

Current state: **16 of 16 reviews produced normally.** Nothing has ever
failed, which is worth knowing rather than assuming.

**Evidence for the reference data.** Rejection notes and checklist answers are
the exact material that moves a requirement from `verified: false` to
verified, and both were landing in columns nobody read. Now collected in one
place, so every submission adds to the reference data instead of producing one
email and being forgotten.

### It paid for itself immediately

The harvest surfaced a real rejection already sitting in the database:

> "Lockout/tagout program does not include periodic inspection requirements"

From a scaffolding contractor, both platforms, July. That is the first piece
of first-hand evidence in this project — everything else in
`lib/requirements` came from consultant checklists.

The lockout/tagout entry now says to check how often procedures are inspected,
and cites the submission it came from. Still `verified: false`: one reviewer
on one submission sharpens the advice, it does not confirm the requirement.

## Task 061 — Incident Reporting and Investigation

The fourth programme, and the last of the planned set. Built exactly like the
other three: one template file, one line in the registry, no change to the
questionnaire, the validator, the renderers or the storage.

### What it is built around

`lib/requirements` reduces this requirement to one sentence: check it says
who is told, in what order, and what gets written down. That is the whole
document, so the three load-bearing parts are:

- **A numbered notification ladder.** Not prose mentioning the same people in
  a sentence — an ordered list. The host site's control room and the client's
  contact are *in* it, in position. That is the part written for
  subcontractors specifically: a chain that ends at the owner describes a
  company that works alone, and the host usually has a contractual window to
  be told.
- **A table of the fields every incident record carries**, ending with
  "closed out by" — who confirmed the corrective action was actually done.
- **Corrective actions with an owner, a date, and that check.** An action
  marked complete without it is not closed out.

Near misses are reportable and are said to be the ones worth the most. That
is the gap most often found in a subcontractor's programme: a procedure that
only starts once somebody is already hurt.

### The deadline that is not written down

Serious injuries are reportable to OSHA on a clock. The numbers are easy to
half-remember and a wrong one printed in somebody's own safety programme is
worse than saying nothing — it is checkable, and it will be checked.

So the document states no clock. It commits to reporting on the same day the
Company learns of the event, and to confirming the current requirement at the
point of making the report. A commitment stricter than any deadline stays
true whatever the deadline turns out to be. A test enforces it, matching on
"within N hours" and "within N days".

30 tests, all negative. Release stays `internal_testing` pending a prose
review, same as the Emergency Action Plan.

### A real rendering defect, found by reading the output

The running header and footer draw at 8pt grey. Page breaks happen in the
middle of a block — the break is triggered between a caller setting its font
and that caller writing its text. The cursor was restored afterwards and the
fill colour was restored, but the type was not.

So **the first block on every new page rendered in 8pt footer type**. Correct
content, correct structure, and a document a contractor forwards to a hiring
client with one paragraph a third smaller than the one above it. This has
affected all four programmes since the renderer was written.

Every structural test passed throughout, because the section tree was never
at fault. The two new tests inflate the PDF's content streams and read the
font size each run was set in — and they run against a fixture engineered to
straddle a page break, because the Hazard Communication programme passes them
either way: none of its blocks happened to land on a boundary. Testing on it
would have been testing on a coincidence.

**223 tests pass, lint and build clean.**

### The reference data disagreed with itself about how to spell "program"

The twelve original entries said "Hazard communication programme". The
fourteen added earlier today said "Electrical safety program". So a single
review listed both spellings, to a US industrial contractor — and the intake
checkbox for the very same requirement already said "program".

Six labels normalized, plus one action that said "energised work is
authorised". Two new tests: one rejects British spelling in any label, action
or checklist, the other checks that the label a customer reads shares words
with the checkbox they ticked, since seeing a different name come back means
nobody can tell they are the same document. `phrases` stays exempt — those
search the contractor's own file, where either spelling may genuinely appear.

Reviews already in the database carry the old labels. `matchesLabel` on
HazCom and PPE already accepted both spellings, and that was verified against
the join rather than assumed: an old review still resolves to the right
program, so the "prepare this" action does not vanish from anybody's history.

### One of these was not a spelling slip

The unreadable-scan email told people to look for a button called
**"Recognise Text"**. In US Adobe Acrobat that button is *Recognize Text* — so
the instruction sent somebody hunting for a control that does not exist under
that name, in the one email we send to people whose upload we could not read.
Fixed in both the HTML and plain-text versions.

**225 tests pass, lint and build clean.**
