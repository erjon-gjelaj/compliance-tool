
## Ten programs instead of four

Tier 1 of the program catalog is the ten every contractor is asked for
regardless of trade. Four existed. The other six now do: Job Safety Analysis,
Safety Training & Orientation, Disciplinary & Enforcement, Safety Policy,
Injury & Illness Recordkeeping, and Drug & Alcohol.

### A shared spine, and why the original four do not use it

Open any real safety program and the first two sections and the last three are
close to interchangeable — purpose, scope, who looks after it, what employees
are told, where it is kept, when it is reviewed. Writing fifty-six variants of
"Availability of This Program" adds nothing and guarantees they drift. So
`spine.ts` owns the frame and each program owns its subject.

The original four are deliberately not retrofitted. They are released,
customers hold versions of them, and re-assembling released prose to share a
helper would change the bytes of documents somebody has already submitted to a
hiring client, for no benefit to them.

### The two written most carefully

**Drug & Alcohol** is an employment policy, not a safety procedure, and that
is where a generated document can do real legal harm: testing law varies by
state, several states restrict action over lawful off-duty conduct, and
commercial drivers sit under a separate federal regime. So it states the
commitment — which is genuinely the company's to make and the part a hiring
client is asking about — and refers the mechanics of testing to counsel, the
client's contract, and the law where the work is done. It names no substance,
no cut-off, no panel, and no consequence expressed as a legal entitlement.

**Recordkeeping** sits next to rules about which injuries go on a log and
which events are reportable on a clock. It restates none of them. It is a
procedure — who decides, by when, against what — and it says in the document
that it is not a substitute for the current rules. A contractor whose program
instead contains a summary of the rules is carrying a summary that will go
stale without telling them.

**Disciplinary** is the one most often written badly. Platforms ask for it and
most contractors supply a ladder of punishments, which is worse than nothing:
a company that reliably punishes people for what goes wrong stops hearing
about what goes wrong, and every other program depends on people reporting
things. It draws the distinction that matters — honest mistake, judgment call,
deliberate choice to skip a control — and only the last is a discipline matter.

### One test file instead of ten

`every-program.test.mts` runs the shared invariants against the registry,
assembling each program twice with its options on and off. Adding a program
puts it under all of them automatically, rather than being as safe as whoever
remembered to copy the checks across.

It immediately found three defects in **already-released** programs:

- HazCom's `matchesLabel` no longer recognized its own configured title, so a
  gap report naming "Hazard Communication" would never have offered to prepare
  it. Invisible from outside — the customer just never sees the offer.
- HazCom said the responsible role "records who has been trained", which reads
  as a claim about people rather than about the record.
- British spellings in documents going to US contractors: "labelled" through
  HazCom, "programme" in PPE and Incident Reporting, "organise" in the EAP.

Both matchers were widened to accept every title the requirement has carried,
and HazCom and PPE went to 1.1.0.

Two pieces of copy went stale the moment the fifth program landed — the
checkout said "all four written programs" and the documents page said only four
had generators. Both now read from the registry.

**315 + 12 tests pass, lint and build clean.**

## The four high-hazard programs

Lockout/Tagout, Confined Space Entry, Hot Work, and Fall Protection. Fourteen
programs offered now, up from four this morning.

These are the first ones where getting the content wrong could hurt somebody
rather than just get a submission rejected, so each is built around the
elements `config/element-sets.json` names — which turn out to be exactly the
things reviewers press on, and mostly the things that kill people.

**Lockout/Tagout** answers the only first-hand rejection this project holds.
The note has been in the database since July, from a scaffolding contractor:
"Lockout/tagout program does not include periodic inspection requirements."
The program has an annual inspection section, and the question asking who
performs it does not offer "the person who did the work" as an option.

Its equipment procedure is a blank table, the same design as the Emergency
Action Plan's site record. A written program cannot contain the isolation
points of machines it has never seen, and the two ways of pretending
otherwise — uselessly generic text, or invented specifics — are bad and much
worse respectively.

**Confined Space** puts the weight on rescue, because most people who die in
confined spaces are would-be rescuers. "Call 911" is not a rescue plan; it is
a phone number and a hope. The program requires the arrangement and its
response time to be written on the permit before entry, and requires retrieval
equipment on the entrant so a rescue can start without anybody going in. The
test order — oxygen, then flammable, then toxic — is stated with the reason,
because a combustible-gas reading taken in a low-oxygen atmosphere is wrong in
the direction that gets somebody hurt.

**Hot Work** sets a fire watch that outlasts the work, and requires a second
check after the watch ends. Fires from hot work characteristically start after
the torch goes off: a spark travels, lodges out of sight, smoulders, and finds
air later. The document also says a fire watch holding a ladder is not a fire
watch.

**Fall Protection** states the clearance arithmetic in plain terms, because
that is the calculation crews do not do at all — a lanyard that arrests a fall
below the surface has not saved anybody. Rescue is required to work in minutes,
since a harness stops the fall and then becomes the hazard.

None of them states a trigger height, a standard, or a citation. Construction
and general industry are treated differently on several of these subjects, this
project has deliberately never verified a universal counterpart, and a template
printing one number would be wrong for somebody.

### The test found a weakness in itself

The leak check compares document prose against every answer id, and LOTO's
`inspector` tripped it — legitimately, since "inspector" is also an ordinary
word. Worse, `meetings` in the Safety Policy had been passing only because the
prose happened to capitalize the word at the start of a sentence.

So answer ids now have to contain an underscore, which makes them unmistakably
internal and lets the leak check be strict. Five ids added today were renamed;
HazCom's `labelling` is grandfathered, because it is written into the stored
answers of every version already issued and renaming it would make a revision
of one of those documents fail as a contradictory answer.

The spelling check also grew the doubled-consonant family after "cancelled"
reached a rendered confined-space permit.

**316 + 12 tests pass, lint and build clean.**

## Tier 2 complete — thirty programs

Tiers 1 and 2 are done: the thirty programs a contractor in this trade is
actually asked for. Tier 3 is the specialised thirty (excavation, silica,
cranes, H2S and so on), and those apply to some contractors and not others.

The ones worth noting for what they refuse to do:

**Fleet and Driver Safety** is where a small contractor's people are most
likely to be killed, and it is almost always the thinnest document in their
file — it happens off site, between jobs, so it does not feel like work. The
content is the three things that actually kill crews: phones, fatigue at the
end of a long shift, and the schedule that produced the drive. The phone rule
is written as an obligation on the office as much as the driver, because a
rule that leaves somebody choosing between the policy and their supervisor's
call is not a rule.

**Return to Work** names the incentive problem in the document itself.
Bringing somebody back on light duties helps them and also keeps a case off
the lost-time count; those point the same way most of the time and in opposite
directions occasionally, and the occasion is the one that matters. A program
that does not acknowledge that is not credible to anybody who has seen it
happen.

**Safety Observations** is titled from the config, which calls it
behavior-based safety, but the content deliberately does not adopt the
framing. Counting unsafe acts by workers locates every problem in the person
nearest the sharp end, and a crew that learns observations produce findings
about them stops behaving normally while anybody is watching — at which point
the program measures nothing while producing a healthy number of observations.
Two rules keep it alive: nothing recorded against a name, and no target for
the count.

**Management of Change** is written for the change at ten in the morning
rather than as a process-plant procedure. A scaffolding contractor is not
running a process unit, and a document written as though they were is one they
will never open.

**Fire Prevention** states the order — alarm, out, call — and says nobody is
ever required to use an extinguisher. Most contractor fire programs read as
though every employee is expected to fight one, which puts somebody between a
fire and the exit while they decide.

Also added: Hand and Power Tools, Housekeeping, Hearing Conservation,
Bloodborne Pathogens, Ladder Safety, Heat Illness, Stop Work Authority,
Subcontractor Management.

### Test corrections

Three, all cases of a check being wrong rather than the prose:

- "specialist" was flagged as British. It is correct American English; only the
  verb forms are not.
- "licence" was not flagged and should have been. American English uses
  "license" for both the noun and the verb.
- paperwork.test.mts named ladder safety as its example of a program with no
  generator and broke the day ladder safety got one. It now asks the config.

**316 + 12 tests pass, lint and build clean.**

## Trade-specific programs, chosen rather than enumerated

Tier 3 is thirty specialised programs, all triggered by a declared scope of
work rather than applying to everybody. Building all thirty mechanically would
mean writing for trades this platform does not serve, so this batch covers the
ones the five trades in the intake actually trigger: Scaffolding, High-Pressure
Water Blasting, Asbestos, Aerial Lifts, and Silica.

**Asbestos refuses to produce a document for half its askers.** A contractor
who removes asbestos is doing licensed work — accreditation, notification,
enclosures, clearance monitoring, medical surveillance — and nothing generated
from a questionnaire could serve as their program. So the first question asks
whether they abate, and if they do, the document says plainly that this is not
their program and points at where the real procedures are held. Producing
something that looked like an abatement program would be worse than producing
nothing, because it would sit in a file where a crew might take it for the real
thing.

For everybody else — insulators, mechanical, electrical — it says the thing
that matters: assume it is asbestos until somebody competent establishes it is
not, stop, leave it, keep people out, report. And it keeps the exposure record
for a very long time, because the disease appears decades later and nobody else
will have kept it.

**Water blasting** is built around the injection injury. A jet can go through
skin leaving a mark like a pin prick, the wound closes, the person says they
are fine, and the damage develops over hours — people lose limbs to injuries
they described at the time as nothing. So any contact means hospital
immediately, the crew is told the exact words to say to the medical staff, and
nobody is questioned for going over a mark that turns out to be nothing. The
other half is that the dead-man control is never defeated, which it always is,
because holding it is tiring.

**Aerial Lifts** keeps apart the two harness rules most programs collapse into
one. A harness in a boom lift keeps somebody in the basket, because booms whip
and that is what kills people in them. A harness in a scissor lift does not do
the same job, because a scissor lift tips rather than whips, and being tethered
to a machine going over is not protection.

**Silica** says the dust that causes the disease is too small to see and the
visible cloud is the harmless part — which is why a crew who waited for it to
settle believe they have dealt with it. The control runs for the whole task or
it has not run, and a respirator is used as well as it and never instead,
because everybody else in the area is otherwise unprotected.

**Scaffolding** separates the competent person from the qualified person, which
contractors routinely collapse into "the foreman" — and a reviewer reads that
collapse as the company not knowing the difference.

None of them states a height, a pressure, a distance, an exposure limit, or a
clearance. Those come from the equipment's own plate, the manufacturer's data,
or the applicable standard, and a number printed in a generated document would
compete with the one on the actual machine.

**316 + 12 tests pass, lint and build clean.**
